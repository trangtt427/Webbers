Squarespace

Leveraging an AI-forward development process, I led the evolution of Acuity Scheduling beyond 1:1 appointments, bringing in a new era of class scheduling and tapping into an underserved market.

Role
Design Lead
Interim PM
Timeline
8 months
Key Results
100% of new users since August are now on the improved class-scheduling experience
Problem Context

The current class offering was not built to help customers scale sessions, and forced them to create classes without seeing what the rest of their schedule looks like.

Acuity Scheduling was originally built for 1:1 appointments, with classes added later haphazardly and following the same pattern of appointment creation. Fitness studios, yoga instructors, and educators were left with an experience that was cobbled together and not designed around their class-based workflows. Creating classes lacked calendar context, and editing recurring classes required updating each session individually.

Strategy

With traditional processes being challenged by AI, I used the opportunity to build an elaborate prototype with Claude Code. This was presented to Leadership and was greenlit to be our focus of the year.

While this vision prototype was spun up in just a week, it was informed by prior months of user research sessions and customer support stories. It was also the first time we started the product development process this way. Using AI tools accelerated the discovery process, got prototypes in front of people for user testing quickly, exposed edge cases early, and made it easier for stakeholders to visualize the end goal and the in-betweens needed to get there.

Building out the class vision shown in the prototype would be a massive multi-quarter effort, so I had to prove that we could deliver incremental value by breaking it into smaller milestones.

With support from the EM, I proposed 3 tracks of work that would mirror the lifecycle of how class-based businesses use our product:

Class creation experience.

Class management experience.

Client booking experience.

Each subsequent track of work would build off of the previous, greatly improving the overall experience but still allowing just enough flexibility for pivots and reprioritizations.

Concepting

The project already kicked off with a feature-rich prototype using most of Squarespace's design language, so the concepting phase was all about refinement—using exact components, tackling complex scheduling edge cases, and planning the rollout to new and existing users.

There was a lot of experimentation during this phase. It was the first time engineers worked outside of Figma to understand the flows, which had its issues. I tried to close the gaps as best I could with proprietary tools for annotating and viewing "all artboards"—quality of life things that we're used to in Figma.

My concept pushed a distinction that users were already making in their heads: Standalone Classes vs. Series Classes.

A Standalone Class — a one-time or recurring session that clients can join without committing to all. Ex. a weekly Yoga class.

A Series Class — a multi-session class scheduled as a set. Ex. a 6-week pilates course.

This was an important shift in how these two class types were presented. The current experience allowed users to switch between the two at any point, creating negative downstream effects in payment and client expectations. These are meant to be two separate products with distinct uses.

For users offering recurring classes, the current experience made it impossible to edit in bulk. I introduced edits at the class level, where changes here propagate into individual sessions.

Our research sessions taught us that people wanted to see what the rest of their schedule looked like before committing to times during class setup.

We responded with making class creation to happen in the Calendar UI, the most natural place for users. Users can edit class sessions directly from here, or they can go to the Services page where they can make class level changes.

For months, we relied on the Claude prototype to build off of. After concerns from engineers about its tradeoffs, I made the decision to jump back into Figma for sake of better collaboration and progress.

The development team had trouble figuring out from the prototypes what were MVP and was Vision. With Figma, you'd have explicit documentation calling out which artboards are associated with each, which would lead to more engagement from engineers. My proprietary comment tools and markdown files couldn't replace that.

Implementation

During development, we ended up omitting a lot of the niceties that I included in the vision prototype, while making some drastic reprioritization decisions.

We were already behind on schedule so I made some prioritization calls to drop some of the features I initially scoped out. While these extra functionalities really impressed early stakeholders, they weren't necessary to the jobs to be done. Everyone was in agreement and we moved them to post-MVP items.

Because the project took longer than expected, Leadership wanted us to shift our focus to smaller projects of high value right after delivering the MVP, which meant putting the class management experience aside for now.

We put what we had ready into production, and opened it to just the internal team and CustOps to initiate a bug bash.

With all hands on deck, we identified about two dozen bugs, which our entire team of engineers swarmed on for the next week. I also made a few design changes based on some of that feedback (mainly around messaging).

Once we were comfortable for the product to be out in the wild, we put it into production for 1% of new users. No issues cropped up, so we immediately ramped up to 100%.

Outcome

After one month of delivering the new class experience, we saw remarkable numbers: 33% increase in class creation with an outpour of positive feedback delivered to us via CustOps support tickets.
